MPs split on the cable’s price: from 8 cents less per kilowatt-hour to a 10–15% higher bill

At Thursday’s plenary, Disy, Diko and Elam backed the Great Sea Interconnector, while Akel, Alma and Direct Democracy demanded figures. The forecasts MPs offered for the electricity price point in opposite directions.

CyNews 24 Editorial TeamPublished: 20 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)Updated: 20 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)

On Thursday, 19 November, during the House plenum’s discussion of Chapter D, the parties split over the Great Sea Interconnector, the electricity cable planned between Cyprus and Greece. Economy Today reported that Disy, Diko and Elam spoke in favour, while Akel, Alma and Direct Democracy entered reservations, citing a lack of data and transparency. Some MPs warned that the bill would fall on consumers with no guarantee of cheaper power. Others defended the cable as the way to end the island’s energy isolation. The figures below are what MPs said, as Economy Today reported them, not a single official estimate. Where the project itself stands is in the 2031 timetable article.

Diko leader Nicholas Papadopoulos said electricity in Greece is 30% cheaper than in Cyprus, and that if the cable already existed, power coming from Greece through the GSI would be 30% cheaper, on average 8 cents per kilowatt-hour. He put the benefit at €180 million for Cypriot households and businesses over the next 30 years. “These are the benefits of the GSI,” he said. Natural gas and the cable, he said, are the only ways both to cut the cost and to keep electricity adequate in the coming years: “The rest is detail.” The charge on the consumer, he said, would be 1 cent per kilowatt-hour. “The GSI fee has been set by the European operator and does not change, whether the cable costs €1.9 billion or €2.4 billion. What can be extended is the payback period.” He asked how an investment that has European Commission funding and the backing of the French group Meridiam can be doubted.

Disy leader Annita Demetriou said Cyprus remains the only EU country without an electricity link to another country, and called the GSI a project of strategic importance. Even if it costs €2.5 billion, she said, the sum would come down to about €1.9 billion because of €657 million in European funding. “With 15 to 20 years to pay it off, there is no way the cost of electricity will be higher than the 29 cents per kilowatt-hour we pay today.” For Disy, she said, the link is a matter of energy security, European integration and European strategy, and she accused the government of a lack of decisiveness: “And this is not the first time.”

Akel general secretary Stefanos Stefanou accepted the project’s strategic weight and immediately questioned its viability and the effect on consumers. The cost, he said, started at €1.2 billion and may now reach as much as €3 billion. The worst case, he said, is to pay for a project that is never built. The second worst is to pay for many years for a cable that is finished without the consumer benefit some people promise. “Without data, some say it will bring cheaper electricity. Did the price fall in the EU?” He accused the government of committing Cyprus to payments of hundreds of millions while the cost, the timetable and who will control the cable remain unanswered. “Others decide for us, without us, but we are already paying the bill, which will cost billions.” Strategic importance, he added, is not a blank cheque.

Odysseas Michaelides, leader of Alma, said that on their calculations, if the cost reaches or exceeds €3 billion, the project could well raise the electricity price by 10 to 15%. He called the GSI another scandal of the Anastasiades government and laid responsibility on President Christodoulides as well. In August 2023, when he was auditor-general, he said he had warned the European Commission that the implementing body of the time was not up to the job and had proposed an open selection of a new one. “Its CV contained company bankruptcies and bounced cheques.” He accused the Christodoulides government of awarding the project directly to the Greek operator Admie without a tender, and said the previous body received €48 million “to sell the project’s hot air”. “Officials of the Nicos Anastasiades government were employed there on fat salaries.” He also criticised the decision to recover €125 million even if the project is not built, and cited Finance Minister Makis Keravnos on the need for viability studies before large investments.

Elam MP Marios Pelekanos said the cable should not be judged on money alone: it is a project of the highest geopolitical importance and would end energy isolation. He repeated that the country remains the only one in the EU without an interconnected grid, and asked where Cyprus would be if it had had a link with a neighbouring country since 2013. Direct Democracy MP Yiannis Laouris proposed three tests for the GSI: energy adequacy, a lower electricity price, and national security. “If we are going to lose €125 million now, better to lose it now than to lose billions in the future.” On his reading, the cable does not guarantee cheaper power, and the estimates that exist show the opposite, a rise in cost. “We are talking about a huge investment. And people do not know whether electricity will be more expensive or cheaper for the investment the public will pay for. For me that is absurd. I do not understand why the public has to pay for a private investment.”
