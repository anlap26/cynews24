Pension bill is held at the Legal Service over benefits for Europeans who worked in Cyprus

The 1 January 2027 target still stands, but the minister told parliament on Monday that covering non-residents would mean about €100 million the funds cannot bear.

CyNews 24 Editorial TeamPublished: 13 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)Updated: 16 November 2026, 16:00 (EET)

Labour Minister Marinos Mousiouttas says the target of putting the pension reform into effect on 1 January 2027 still stands, even though the bill has been delayed. He spoke on a morning radio programme on Friday.

The hold-up stems from a legal obstacle that the ministry is trying to resolve together with the Legal Service. Mousiouttas expects it to be overcome in the coming days, after which the bill would go to the Cabinet and then to parliament.

On early retirement at 63, the reform would cut the actuarial reduction from 12 per cent to 7.5 per cent of the basic pension, for existing pensioners and those who retire over the next five years. Incentives are also planned for people who choose to keep working until 67.

Meanwhile, at the 17th Pension Forum, most of the technical experts who spoke came out in favour of making provident funds compulsory, according to Phileleftheros.

On Monday 16 November, at the House finance committee during the Labour Ministry's 2027 budget, Mousiouttas said the bill is now with the Legal Service. The way the benefits are drawn up, he said, suggests they might also have to be paid to people who worked in the Republic but do not live here — for example EU citizens who spent some years in Cyprus and would qualify on the same terms as a Cypriot. "It is €100 million of increase that the funds will not withstand. If we put these in, we will have to take them from somewhere else," he told Reporter. The ministry is looking for an answer that is lawful and does not open the way to wider claims. Relations with the social partners are good, he added, but there are disagreements on the money.

The same morning, at the Labour Advisory Board, the minister put forward a new scheme for provident funds: if the target for wider coverage is not met, they would be made compulsory by law. Companies that join only once the funds are compulsory would not get the same incentives as companies that join from the start, while the second pillar is still voluntary.

In a note Mousiouttas submitted to the committee — Politis reports its contents — pension increases run from 5% to 60% and would be paid in stages over three years: 30% in the first year, 30% in the second and 40% in the third. For 15 years the amount would be compared with the current system, so that nobody receives less than they would now. Existing Social Insurance Fund pensioners whose pension is up to €600 are guaranteed a minimum increase of €30 a month. The note estimates that about 123,000 existing pensioners would be affected; more than 50,000 would gain over €100 a month and more than 10,000 over €200. It also sets out a new contribution obligation for people with income.

On the second pillar, Politis writes that the unions SEK, PEO and DEOK generally want universal compulsory cover, while the employers' organisations OEB and KEVE want the voluntary character kept. The minister, citing the specialists, said putting provident funds in place would take three to four years.
