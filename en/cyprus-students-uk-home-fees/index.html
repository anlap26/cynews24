EU wants Cypriot students in Britain to pay home university fees

Before Friday’s EU–UK summit, Brussels wants European students to pay no more than British students. UK fees are up to £9,790; others can pay up to £35,000 or more.

CyNews 24 Editorial TeamPublished: 19 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)Updated: 19 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)

The European Union will go into Friday’s summit with the United Kingdom hoping that European students at British universities pay no more than British students, the Cyprus Mail reported on Thursday. The paper understands that this is the intention when European Council President Antonio Costa and Commission President Ursula von der Leyen sit down with Prime Minister Andy Burnham.

British students now pay a maximum of £9,790 a year for an undergraduate course, which the Mail converts as €11,748. Students who are not British can pay as much as £35,000 (€41,998) a year for a classroom degree, up to £47,000 (€56,398) for a laboratory course, and sometimes more than £70,000 (€83,997) for a clinical or medical degree. Until the UK left the EU and the single market in 2020, citizens of the other 27 member states, and of Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway and Switzerland, could pay the home fee. That ended with the departure. Since then, the paper says, the more than 20,000 Europeans who enrol at British universities each year have had to pay many times more than British students on the same courses.

Cypriot MEP Loucas Fourlas, of Disy, who sits on the European Parliament’s culture and education committee, complained in January of “endless fees” for Europeans in Britain. Education, he said, “cannot be transformed to a tool of exclusion, nor can it be collateral damage in geopolitical tensions”. If Britain wants to re-examine its relationship with the EU, he added, the relationship cannot be one-sided, and cutting fees for European students would be a tangible step.

The UK has so far looked reluctant to restore home fees in full. Euronews reported on Wednesday, the Mail writes, that a draft statement for the end of Friday’s summit contains two options. One is “equal treatment” for EU nationals. The other goes only as far as “significantly reduced” undergraduate fees.

The Guardian, as cited by the Mail, reported that letting European students pay home fees could cost the British government as much as £450 million a year, which the Mail puts at €540 million, and that London is willing to consider it if Britain is allowed into the “Made in EU” scheme. That preference comes from the industrial accelerator act the Commission proposed in March. If it became law, the Mail says, firms based outside the EU could lose access to public contracts inside the bloc.

A separate “youth experience scheme”, first proposed by the Commission in 2024, is not set for 2030, Euronews reported, according to the Mail. The aim is an agreement in 2028. The UK would cap participants at 80,000 and the EU at 100,000. The scheme, as now planned, would be for people aged 18 to 30, which the paper notes would leave those who were children at the Brexit referendum too old to use it.
