Avakoum monastery trial: a witness put unreceipted collections at €3.62 million

On Friday the head of the tax-fraud investigation unit told the Nicosia permanent criminal court how the service calculated the Osios Avvakoum monastery’s collections and a tax bill of €285,067.49. The hearing resumes on 30 November.

CyNews 24 Editorial TeamPublished: 21 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)Updated: 21 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)

On Friday the hearing in the Osios Avvakoum monastery case continued at the Nicosia permanent criminal court. The first prosecution witness, George Georgiou, head of the tax-fraud investigation unit, set out what the tax department had found. Kathimerini, citing the Cyprus News Agency, and the Cyprus Mail both reported the evidence.

Georgiou said the service had recorded collections of €3,620,607 for which no lawful receipts were issued. He also gave figures drawn from bank accounts for 2018 to 2024: deposits of €4,857,356, cash payments of €1,264,162, and receipts issued of €738,969. “The calculation determines the amount of collections for which no receipt was issued, and it comes to €3,620,607,” he said. Asked by prosecution counsel Theodora Papakyriakou, he added that the service had examined the bank data but had not used them in the calculation behind the tax assessment.

According to the tax-assessment letter, as he put it to the court, the Avakoum church owes €285,067.49 for the period from 1 September 2021 to 31 March 2024, plus charges and interest. He said the inquiry was meant to establish whether the books and records had serious gaps that would justify refusing the returns that had been filed. The service found such gaps and made its own calculation of the taxable amount.

At the time of the inquiry the monastery had filed returns only for 1 September 2021 to 31 March 2022, and those were late. After the audit, returns covering the whole period were also filed late. He said that did not change the result: because of the gaps in the books, the returns were not accepted. Only income and sales that had already been declared were subtracted from the calculation of undeclared sales.

He explained what the service meant by “arbitrary returns”. An Excel file had the amount to be declared set in advance, and the blanks were then filled with names from a list in the same file. “The right course would have been to record every receipt, if receipts were issued, or every sales invoice, and for that total to be the amount in the return. Instead, what we saw — and this was our conclusion at first sight — was that they decided what amount to enter,” he said.

On the commercial side he put expected sales from the monastery’s exhibition, pastry shop and store at €1,475,074. The returns declared €243,958. The difference was €1,231,116. Judged by the level of sales, he said, the monastery could be classed as a small or medium-sized enterprise. He calculated undeclared output tax on further sales from the exhibition, the store, ready-made goods and pastry at €125,450.40, and called that the largest calculation for the monastery: eleven subjects, each with its own method.

Commissions on excursions, he said, came from work with Loucas Travel and Tours, trading as Nazoreos Travel. Material received from the police included cheque numbers and transfers to the monastery. Undeclared output tax on those commissions was €15,974.37. Asked whether such commissions are taxed, he said every commission is taxed at the ordinary VAT rate.

A further €143,642.74, he said, was output tax on civil-engineering services the monastery received, under article 11B. The sum, he said, came mainly from contractors’ invoices for building and renovation. Asked whether monasteries have any VAT exemption, he answered: “As regards VAT legislation, there is no exemption for any taxpayer.”

The prosecution asked whether the personal returns of the first defendant, former archimandrite Nektarios, known in lay life as Ioannis Georgiou, had been checked. Georgiou said the check covered everyone involved in the case. “We had findings, however, only for the first defendant.”

The inquiry began with a police letter to the tax commissioner, asking for documents the police held to be examined and for a view on whether a tax offence arose. The team read papers at police offices, went to the Metropolis of Tamassos, where it collected further material, and to the monastery’s premises. It also inspected a pastry and bakery workshop the monastery kept in a neighbouring village. The report does not name the village. The team contacted the metropolis’s auditor, the head of the Church of Cyprus audit office, and suppliers.

Georgiou heads the unit and has worked at the department for eight years. He gave a statement to the police on 28 November 2024. On 13 June 2024 a preliminary note went to investigators on whether, at first sight, offences under the tax laws arose. The unit’s report is dated 20 November 2024. It was shown to the court with the tax assessment and its annexes.

The Cyprus Mail wrote that on Thursday the court heard a tradesman say he was paid €2,450 in cash without an invoice, after being told it would be a shame for the monastery to pay VAT. That was Thursday’s evidence, not part of Georgiou’s account on Friday.

The hearing resumes on 30 November 2026 at 9:30, when the defence is to cross-examine the witness. Kathimerini notes that every defendant is presumed innocent until the court finds guilt beyond reasonable doubt.
