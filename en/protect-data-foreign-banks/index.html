How to protect your data and accounts when using foreign banks and services

A foreign bank is not, by itself, less safe than the one you left. The risk is the login, the email that can reset it, and the phone number that receives the code, set up on a network you do not control.

Moving country changes the paperwork. It does not change what a criminal wants. The valuable objects are the bank login, the email that can reset it, the phone number that receives the code, and the tax portal that already knows your identity number. A foreign bank is not, by itself, less safe than the one you left. The new risk is that you are setting this up while you are tired, on a network you do not control, with a SIM that may stop receiving texts.

This guide is about that practical problem. It does not quote a share of fraud, because a percentage we cannot point to a source for does not belong here. It is not a set of instructions for getting past a bank’s checks. Those checks exist so the bank knows who it is paying. Hiding your location from your own bank is a good way to have the account frozen.

The pair to this page is the health-insurance breakdown. An assistance line you cannot reach, and a mailbox you no longer open, fail in the same way as a bank you cannot log in to.

What a VPN does, and what it does not

A virtual private network encrypts the traffic between your device and the VPN company’s server. It can hide that traffic from the café, hotel or airport network you are sitting on. That is a real use, and a narrow one.

A VPN does not make you anonymous to your bank. The bank still sees an account holder logging in. It does not stop a fake website. If you type a password into a page that only looks like the bank, the password still goes to whoever built the page. It does not clean a phone that already has malicious software on it. And it can make the bank’s own defences treat the login as suspicious, because the connection appears to come from a country you are not in. People get locked out that way, often on the day a transfer has to go through.

Do not use a VPN to pretend to your bank that you are somewhere else. If the bank asks you to say when you travel, say so. For the banking session itself, the cleaner path is the bank’s own app on mobile data from a SIM the bank already knows. A free VPN, installed from an advert, whose owner you cannot name, is a poor place to put that session. This page will not recite a particular scandal as if it were a statistic. The rule is enough: choose the tool before you need it, from a company you can identify.

NordVPN, Surfshark and Proton VPN

NordVPN, Surfshark and Proton VPN are commercial VPN services with apps for ordinary phones and computers. They are not banks, and they are not a guarantee that a transfer will succeed. This page does not rank them, does not quote a price, and does not repeat claims about how many servers they run or what an audit found. Those claims sit on their own sites and they change. Read the current description of what they say they log, where the company sits, and which apps they actually ship.

Use one you installed before the trip, and keep it updated, for ordinary browsing on a network you did not choose. For the banking app, prefer mobile data, and turn the VPN off if the bank rejects the session. A tool that locks you out is not protecting the account.

The email account is the master key

Banks, tax portals and insurers all offer a “forgot password” path, and it leads to an inbox. If that inbox is an old address with a password you also used somewhere else, the bank is only as strong as the inbox.

Give money its own email address if you can, one you will still control after you change country. Turn on the strongest sign-in the provider offers, and store the backup codes somewhere that is not only the phone. Proton Mail is one service built around encrypted mail. It is not the only serious option, and it does not stop you clicking a convincing fake. It does put the mailbox with a company whose product is the mailbox, which is clearer than a free address whose recovery phone you no longer remember.

The recovery address and the recovery phone are part of the lock. If both of them die when you land, you have scheduled a lockout. Change them before you travel, while the old codes still arrive.

Passwords that survive a move

Reusing the password of your home bank on a new bank, on email and on a tax portal is the failure that does not care which country issued the card. A password manager stores unique passwords in an encrypted vault. 1Password is one such manager. The price and the current features are on its own site. They are not copied here.

The habit is the point. A different password for the bank, for the email that resets the bank, and for the tax portal. The vault locked. The master password not left where a stranger can use it, and not stored only inside the email account the vault is meant to protect. An emergency kit, which is how a serious manager lets a locked-out owner back in, kept offline.

The browser’s save-password prompt on a hotel business-centre computer is not a manager. Do not use a shared computer for a bank. If you have no choice, do not save the password, sign out, and change it later from your own device.

Codes, SIM cards and a number that changes

Text-message codes are the second factor many banks still use. They fail in three ordinary ways when you move. Roaming blocks the text. You replace the SIM and the bank still has the old number. Or someone persuades the mobile company to move your number onto their SIM, and the codes go to them.

Where the bank allows an authenticator app or a hardware key, that survives a change of number better than a text. Set it up before you leave, and write down the backup codes. Keep them with the password manager’s emergency kit, not only in a camera roll on the same phone.

If the bank allows only texts, keep the old SIM alive until the bank has accepted the new number. Change the number inside the bank’s own app, or by calling the number the bank publishes. Do not do it through a link in an email. A travel eSIM is often data only. The health-insurance guide makes the same point about an assistance line: do not assume a data plan receives the text your bank sends to the number on file.

Pages that look like the bank

The durable attack is a message. It says the account is limited, a tax return is late, or a payment needs you to “confirm”. The link is one character off the real domain. The page asks for the password and then for the code.

The UK National Cyber Security Centre’s tips for staying secure online, and the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency’s note on strong passwords, land on the same habits: unique passwords, and caution with messages you did not expect. Neither page is a Cyprus manual. The habit travels.

Type the address yourself, or use the official app, installed from the official store before the trip. Do not install remote-control software because a caller told you to. Do not read a one-time code aloud. A bank that already holds the account does not need you to hand over the code that proves you are holding the phone.

Cyprus individual returns go through the Tax Department’s own route. The deadline story is about that calendar. A message that claims to be the Tax For All portal is still just a message. Open the portal the way the department publishes it, not from a shortened link.

Opening an account, and using one you have

The resident handbook points at the Central Bank of Cyprus leaflet on payment accounts with basic features. A consumer legally resident in the European Union has a right to such an account. The leaflet does not give one document list for every bank, and a right to an account is not a right to skip identity checks. Send copies through the bank’s own channel. A person who offers to “help with the file” and wants a passport uploaded to a form you did not ask for is not the bank.

A published average mortgage rate is not your login. The September comparison of housing-loan rates is an ECB statistic. It is not an offer from a lender, and it is not a reason to type a card number into a page that arrived as a message. A fintech firm hiring on the island is a jobs story, in the commerce minister’s remarks. It does not change how you sign in.

When a large provider has an outage, the practical problem is getting back in afterwards. The Microsoft outage report is news about one failure, not a manual. The piece that belongs here is smaller: backup codes, and a second way into the email account, matter on the day the usual app will not open.

A short routine, and the limit of it

Update the phone and the computer before you travel. Turn on a screen lock. Put unique passwords in the manager. Move the bank and the email onto the strongest second factor they allow, and store the backup codes offline. If the bank asks you to register travel, do it in the app. Keep one SIM that still receives texts until the new number is accepted. Choose a VPN, if you want one, before you need the airport network, and switch it off for the bank if the bank then refuses the login. Read the domain before you type a password.

None of that is a product. The services named here are tools for three of the steps: encrypting a connection you do not trust, holding a mailbox, and remembering passwords you no longer reuse. They do not replace the bank’s app, and they do not tell you who an email is really from.

This page will not say that a VPN makes a transfer safe, or that a password manager stops someone who already has your unlocked phone. It will not name a best service, and it will not estimate how often accounts are taken over. If a bank has frozen an account, the bank’s own process is the route, not a setting in a VPN app. This is not legal advice, and it is not a promise that any product prevents fraud.

Questions readers ask

Should I leave a VPN on while I use my bank?

Often, no. A VPN can make the session look as if it comes from another country, and the bank may lock it. Prefer the bank’s own app on mobile data. Use a VPN you chose in advance for browsing on a network you do not trust, and switch it off if the bank refuses the login.

Does a VPN stop phishing?

No. If you type a password into a page that only looks like the bank, the VPN does not take that password back. Read the domain, or open the official app, instead of following a link in a message.

What breaks when I change my phone number?

Text-message codes sent to the old number. Keep the old SIM until the bank has accepted the new one. An authenticator app or a hardware key, where the bank allows it, survives a number change better than a text. Write down the backup codes before you travel.

Is a travel eSIM enough for bank codes?

Often it is not. Many travel eSIMs are data plans and do not receive the text a bank sends to the number it has on file. Check before you rely on one.

Are NordVPN, Surfshark, Proton and 1Password banks?

No. NordVPN and Surfshark sell VPN service. Proton sells a VPN, mail and a password manager as separate products. 1Password is a password manager. None of them is your bank, and none of them guarantees a transfer.

Where do the partner links go?

Each button reads its address from one configuration file. Until a real address is added, the button is a placeholder and does not open a shop. The arrangement is explained on the affiliate disclosure page.
