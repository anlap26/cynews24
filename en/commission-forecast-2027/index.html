Commission: Cyprus growth slows to 3 per cent in 2027, inflation to 2.9

Tuesday's autumn forecast still puts the island well above the eurozone. Higher energy prices, the Commission says, will weigh on spending for a while.

CyNews 24 Editorial TeamPublished: 17 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)Updated: 17 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)

The European Commission published its autumn forecast on Tuesday: the Cyprus economy grows by 3.0 per cent in 2027, after 3.4 per cent this year, and picks up to 3.2 per cent in 2028. Harmonised inflation rises to 3.9 per cent in 2026, then eases to 2.9 per cent in 2027 and 2.4 per cent in 2028. The Cyprus News Agency, Kathimerini, Phileleftheros and the Cyprus Mail all carry those figures. The Commission calls the outlook robust, even though dearer energy is expected to slow growth for a time.

The Cyprus Mail notes that this is an upgrade on May, when the Commission had pencilled in 2.3 per cent this year and 2.7 per cent next year, and had expected 2027 inflation lower, at 2.2 per cent. The paper says 3 per cent in 2027 is two and a half times the eurozone forecast of 1.2 per cent. Output rose 3.2 per cent in the first half. Annual growth slowed to 3.1 per cent in the third quarter from 3.4 per cent in the second. Exports of information and communication services and royalty income kept rising, while tourism receipts fell early in the year as the Middle East conflict unsettled travel.

The Commission expects tourist arrivals to recover fully in 2027 and net services exports to return to their pre-conflict average. For now, the Cyprus Mail reports, hoteliers say advance bookings for next year are running 8 to 12 per cent below comparable levels for 2026, with a clearer picture around the turn of the year. Net exports are forecast to add 0.7 of a percentage point to growth next year after subtracting the same amount in 2026. Domestic demand contributes 2.3 points. Fewer export-oriented foreign companies are expected to move to Cyprus, while technology firms already here expand into more markets.

Cuts to VAT and excise duties have cushioned the energy rise, but the Commission expects higher energy and transport costs to feed into food prices. As inflation eases, automatic cost-of-living adjustments are expected to help restore purchasing power. Compensation per employee rises 5.1 per cent in 2027, from 3.8 per cent this year. Household spending grows 2.8 per cent, down from 4 per cent, and the saving rate rises to 7.6 per cent from 6.3. Unit labour costs rise 2.9 per cent. Unemployment holds at 3.8 per cent and falls to 3.7 per cent in 2028.

Investment growth slows to 2 per cent in 2027 from 7.9 per cent this year, when ship registrations lifted the figure. Excluding those registrations, the Commission expects investment to recover as construction picks up after delays caused by higher costs. The budget surplus is 2.9 per cent of GDP this year, down from 3.6 per cent in 2025. Tax reform, the Commission estimates, reduced revenue by 0.7 per cent of GDP, and energy relief by another 0.4. The Cyprus Mail separately cites preliminary figures from the statistical service: a nine-month surplus of €1.61 billion. The Commission expects the surplus to widen to 3.2 per cent of GDP in 2027 and 3.3 per cent in 2028. Wage indexation takes full effect next year, when extra defence spending financed by SAFE loans is also expected to begin.

Debt fell from a peak of 114 per cent of GDP in 2020 to 52.3 per cent at the end of 2025, below the 60 per cent line for the first time since 2009, Phileleftheros reports. The Commission then puts it at 46.3 per cent in 2026, 40.6 per cent in 2027 and 35.1 per cent in 2028. The current-account deficit stays wide: 10.3 per cent of GDP this year, 9.5 per cent in 2027 and 8.4 per cent in 2028, because of the oil bill and profit transfers by foreign-owned companies. The Commission sees the risks on the downside: a disruption to world trade would hurt shipping, and regional instability and dependence on imported energy remain weaknesses.
