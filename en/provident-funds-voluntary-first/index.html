Provident funds would start voluntary, and could become compulsory in three to four years

Politis and Phileleftheros set out Labour Minister Marinos Mousiouttas’s proposal: bring in 50 to 60 per cent of workers who have no fund now. If that fails, a law would make membership compulsory. The opening contribution is 1.5 per cent each from worker and employer.

CyNews 24 Editorial TeamPublished: 17 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)Updated: 17 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)

Tuesday’s Politis front page is the government’s proposal on provident funds, the second pension pillar. As StockWatch summarises it, firms and workers would join voluntarily at first. The aim is to cover 50 to 60 per cent of people who are outside such funds today. After three to four years the result would be judged. If the target is missed, membership could be made compulsory by law. Papers say firms that join during the voluntary window would get strong offsetting benefits. An intermediate stage, with higher targets and without those opening benefits, is also being discussed.

The opening contribution, Politis reports, is 1.5 per cent from the employer and 1.5 per cent from the worker. Separately, ministers are looking at whether social-insurance contributions would not rise in 2029. Mousiouttas, Phileleftheros writes, wants a fund’s reserve paid to a pensioner monthly rather than as a lump sum. The government does not want incentives to retire at 63. People could keep working until 67, and the pension would rise for it.

Both papers say the social partners still treat the text as a first sketch and want more detail. Unions want compulsory contributions from the start. Employers reject compulsion. The economist Akis Ioannides, quoted by Politis, also wants it to be compulsory and criticises the wider pension design.

Alongside this, the proposal would revise the rules of existing funds, set up an independent supervisor and merge smaller funds so that about 130 remain. The papers and the minister both say the scheme will not move without the trust of workers and employers.

At the same House finance committee sitting, the timetable came up: the pension reform is wanted through a vote before the end of the year. Mousiouttas accepted the time pressure and said an agreement was still possible. He did not take a position on paying pension increases retrospectively if the vote slips. On low pensions he said budget limits, not a choice to leave people under the poverty line, blocked a larger rise and other claims, including payments to widowers. Unions want any link between retirement age and life expectancy to go through social dialogue; the ministry says the existing mechanism already runs automatically. The minister left open a rise in the age after 2028: the next actuarial study is expected in 2028 or 2029 and would follow how life expectancy moves.
