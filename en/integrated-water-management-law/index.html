Parliament unanimously passes a water law: unlicensed abstraction can be stopped, and a backdated permit costs double

The Water Development Department will be able to order a halt to abstraction started without a permit. The fine depends on how serious the breach is and how it affects the resource. MPs passed the law and immediately said the island still has no single water authority.

CyNews 24 Editorial TeamPublished: 20 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)Updated: 20 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)

On Thursday the House of Representatives unanimously passed a new water-management law. Cyprus Mail reported that it will, among other things, empower the Water Development Department to order the termination of water-abstraction projects that begin before a permit is issued. Fines for offences of this kind are to be set “according to the seriousness of the offence and its impact on water resources”, taking into account “the nature, extent, and duration of the violation, the size of the intervention, and the condition of the affected water system”.

At the same time the law allows permits to be issued after the fact, where the department considers that appropriate. The contractor then pays the equivalent of twice the prescribed permit fee. The law also mandates “frequent periodic reviews” of inspections of surface- and groundwater abstraction, and allows the authorities to require “measures to redress damage” if illegal construction or abstraction harms natural watercourses such as streams or rivers.

The paper links the law’s passing to pressure from the European Union. Last year the European Commission threatened to take the Cypriot government to court over its failure to implement the water framework directive. The directive was first implemented in 2000, four years before Cyprus joined the EU. Over the 26 years since, the Commission had taken the view that Cyprus had not fully implemented it, because there were no “periodic reviews” of abstraction permits. In the Commission’s position, as Cyprus Mail reported it: member states must periodically review these control measures, including any permits granted, to see whether they still meet their objectives and, if necessary, update them. “In Cyprus, national legislation does not impose any periodic review, which demonstrates a situation of bad transposition of the directive.” The desalination programme the government described this week is in the article on €200 million and four plants.

The law passed unanimously, but MPs had little praise for it. Diko leader Nicholas Papadopoulos said there is “no law with a more incorrect name”, referring to its official title, the Integrated Water Management Law. “In practice, we do not have unified water management because all issues related to water are fragmented into various services and departments. This is a schizophrenic state policy in a country with a very serious water problem.” The chair of the House agriculture committee, Akel MP Yiannakis Gavriel, said that in a water crisis unlicensed projects undermine the Water Development Department’s ability to extend its control of the situation.

Diko MP Zacharias Koulias said that under the late president Spyros Kyprianou, when the dams were built, the maintenance team had 40 people, and that there are now two. Cyprus Mail notes that it is not true that all of the island’s reservoirs were built under Kyprianou. Koulias linked the present state of things to the Mavrokolympos case and to the loss of millions of cubic metres of water. The paper adds, separately, that the Mavrokolympos reservoir near Paphos was entirely drained in January last year of its 1.4 million cubic metres, and that experts said at the time the pipes, installed in 1966, would have to be replaced.
