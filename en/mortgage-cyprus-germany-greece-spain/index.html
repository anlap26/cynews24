New housing loans in September: Cyprus 3.26%, Germany 4.09%

The ECB average on new euro loans for house purchase in September 2026 was 3.26% in Cyprus, 4.09% in Germany, 3.24% in Greece and 2.99% in Spain. It is not a bank’s offer. The Central Bank of Cyprus caps loan-to-value at 80% for a primary residence and 70% for other property.

CyNews 24 Editorial TeamPublished: 17 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)Updated: 17 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)

The European Central Bank’s average on new housing loans in September 2026 puts Cyprus beside three countries it is often compared with. The annualised agreed rate on new euro loans to households for house purchase, all initial fixation periods together, was 3.26 per cent in Cyprus, 4.09 per cent in Germany, 3.24 per cent in Greece and 2.99 per cent in Spain. Those are the ECB’s MFI interest-rate statistics for new business. They are not one bank’s offer, and they are not a separate series for non-residents.

The series are MIR.M.CY, MIR.M.DE, MIR.M.GR and MIR.M.ES, each with the code .B.A2C.A.R.A.2250.EUR.N. The coverage is loans for house purchase, excluding revolving credit and overdrafts, to households and to non-profit institutions serving households, in euro, at the annualised agreed rate, for new business, across every initial rate fixation. The observations were taken from the ECB Data Portal for the reference month of September 2026. August 2026, on the same series, was 3.31 per cent in Cyprus, 4.01 per cent in Germany, 3.30 per cent in Greece and 3.04 per cent in Spain. A single month moves the figure. The total also mixes fixation periods, and this comparison does not split fixed from floating. Renegotiated loans are a different ECB coverage, the one it calls pure new loans. This comparison is new business. National central banks compile what each country contributes. The figure used here is the one the ECB publishes, so the four countries sit on one definition.

Loan to value is a Cyprus rule with a published ceiling, not a rate. The Central Bank of Cyprus has notified the European Systemic Risk Board of ceilings that bind credit institutions operating in Cyprus: 80 per cent where the facility finances the borrower’s primary residence, and 70 per cent for other property. The ceilings are not written as a test of passport. A non-resident buying a house that is not their primary residence falls in the 70 per cent category if the bank classifies the loan that way. A bank may lend less. The ceiling is not an offer. For Germany, Greece and Spain, the ECB statistics and the central-bank pages checked for this comparison do not publish one official “typical loan-to-value for non-residents”. No such figure is stated for those three countries.

The same guide sets the purchase taxes each country actually publishes beside one another, and it says where a single national rate does not exist. A monthly repayment at a rate a particular bank has offered is a different sum from the September average.
