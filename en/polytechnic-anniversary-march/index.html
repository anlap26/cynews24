53 years after the Polytechnic uprising, Nicosia students march to the US embassy

In Athens the Polytechnic courtyards are open from 9am to 1pm, the buildings stay shut, and more than 5,000 police are posted. The Pancyprian pupil committee gathers at 10:30 at Pasydy and walks to the American embassy.

CyNews 24 Editorial TeamPublished: 17 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)Updated: 17 November 2026, 09:00 (EET)

Tuesday closes the three days of remembrance for the student uprising at the Athens Polytechnic on 17 November 1973, when the junta sent a tank into the grounds and people were killed. The Cyprus Mail and Sigmalive do not give a precise death toll in their morning copy. Both describe today’s arrangements and the Cyprus march.

In Athens the gates open at 9:00 and close at 13:00. The public is allowed into the courtyards; the buildings stay closed. More than 5,000 officers — riot police, DIAS, DRASI, OPKE, security and traffic police — are posted around the Polytechnic, other universities, the US and Israeli embassies, public buildings and Exarchia. Drones and a helicopter feed an operations centre. Checks around the building are continuous. The Cyprus Mail and Sigmalive, the latter citing the Cyprus News Agency, report the same arrangements.

In Nicosia the Pancyprian Coordinating Committee of Pupils (PSEM) gathers at 10:30 at the Pasydy building and marches to the US embassy. Pupils from Nicosia schools and the central pupil councils of the other schools are taking part.

Disy, in a statement, called the anniversary a symbol of struggle and of the defence of democracy, and linked the dictatorship, the 1974 coup in Cyprus and the Turkish invasion that followed: democracy, the party wrote, is never a given. Akel recalled the slogans “Bread, education, freedom” and “US out, Nato out”, and said the people who sent the tanks against the uprising, a few months later and together with EOKA B, turned their weapons on the Republic and opened the way for the invasion. The party called for a fight against the far right, racism and wars, and against turning Cyprus into a base for other countries’ military plans. Diko called 1973 a milestone on the way back to democracy in Greece, and wrote that 53 years on, freedom and dignity are still not self-evident.

This is remembrance and party statements, not a report of clashes: when these pieces were published, neither the Cyprus Mail nor Sigmalive described unrest that had already happened in Athens or in Nicosia.
